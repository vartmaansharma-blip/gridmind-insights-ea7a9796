# Opportunity Tournament — Test 2: Real-World Discovery Kit — 2026-10-11

**Status:** Prepared for execution. No messages have been sent and no customer interviews have occurred. The founder must contact people through legitimate existing relationships or public professional channels and record the actual responses.

## Objective

Within 7 days, determine which of three paths has the strongest real-world signal:
A. GridMind / industrial operations problem
B. Narrow B2B AI service that may become software
C. Creator-/community-led consumer brand

Do not build new product features during this test. This is a problem-and-access test, not a pitch contest.

## Non-negotiable evidence rules

- Record exact observations separately from interpretations.
- Do not claim a customer, partner, endorsement, pilot, result, or saving that has not occurred.
- Do not ask for confidential factory records or personal data. If a buyer wants to share data, first obtain explicit permission and agree scope, storage, access, retention, and deletion.
- Do not send mass spam. Use relevant, respectful outreach and stop if asked.
- Do not approach a company as though representing an established supplier if that is not true.
- For a minor founder, involve a trusted adult for formal commercial commitments, contracts, payments, and any sensitive industrial/site access.
- No onsite factory visit without explicit permission, proper supervision, and the site's safety requirements.
- A polite “sounds interesting” is not willingness to pay.

## Test A — GridMind / industrial operations

### First target profile

Prioritize people who have direct visibility into recurring operational loss and can explain the current workflow:
1. Plant manager / operations head
2. Maintenance or reliability manager
3. Quality manager
4. Production planning manager
5. Factory owner / director at a small or mid-sized manufacturer

Start with **one subsector where a warm introduction exists**. If access is equal, pick a segment with repeated production processes and measurable downtime, scrap/rework, or missed-delivery consequences. Do not assume textile, food, auto components, or any other subsector is best until access and pain are compared.

### Outreach message (warm introduction)

> Hi [Name] — I’m researching how manufacturing teams handle recurring production, quality, or downtime problems. I’m not trying to sell a product in this conversation; I’m trying to understand the current process and where it breaks down. Would you be open to a 15-minute conversation about one recent operational issue and how your team investigated it? I won’t ask for confidential data, and I can keep the discussion general.

### Outreach message (cold but relevant)

> Hello [Name], I’m researching operational problem-solving in manufacturing, especially how teams investigate recurring downtime, scrap/rework, and missed production targets. I’m early in the research and am not claiming to have a validated product. If this falls within your role, would you be willing to share how your team currently handles one recent issue in a short 15-minute research conversation? No confidential information is needed.

### Interview questions (ask about the past, not hypothetical futures)

1. Think about the most recent time an operational issue caused a meaningful loss or delay. What happened?
2. How did you first detect it? Which system, report, spreadsheet, or person surfaced it?
3. Walk me through what happened next, step by step.
4. Who had to participate in finding the cause and deciding what to do?
5. How long did it take to understand the issue and agree on action?
6. What was the consequence—lost output, scrap, rework, overtime, energy, late delivery, or something else? How is it normally measured?
7. How often has a similar issue happened in the last 3–6 months?
8. What did you try? What existing ERP/MES/CMMS/QMS tools, dashboards, consultants, or internal procedures were involved?
9. Where did the current process fail or require manual effort?
10. Who owns the budget or approves a change to this workflow?
11. What evidence would be required to trust a proposed improvement?
12. What data would be needed to investigate this properly, and who can authorize access?
13. Has the company already paid to solve this problem? If yes, what did it pay for and what was missing?
14. What have I not asked that is important?

**Do not ask first:** “Would you use an AI platform?” or “Would you pay for GridMind?” These invite polite speculation. Ask about actual past behaviour and existing spend before discussing a concept.

### Evidence to record
- Date, role, subsector, and whether the conversation was warm/cold.
- Recent incident/workflow in the interviewee's own words.
- Current process and tools.
- Frequency and consequence; label estimates as estimates.
- Budget owner and existing spend.
- Data available, permission path, and sensitivity.
- Alternatives and why they are insufficient.
- Next step voluntarily offered by the interviewee.
- Confidence: high / medium / low, with reason.

## Test B — B2B AI service → software

### Candidate workflow hypotheses (not selected products)
- Repetitive quote/proposal preparation for a defined B2B niche.
- Invoice/order reconciliation across existing systems.
- Recurring compliance/evidence-pack preparation.
- Supplier document collection and follow-up.
- Repetitive management reporting from approved spreadsheets.
- Customer-support or service-ticket triage in a narrow industry.

Pick **one workflow only** after access interviews. Do not build a generic “AI automation agency.”

### Outreach message

> Hi [Name] — I’m researching how [type of business] handles [specific recurring workflow]. I’m trying to understand the current process, what makes it slow or error-prone, and whether the impact is meaningful enough to justify changing it. Could I ask you a few questions in a 15-minute research conversation? I’m not assuming AI is the answer, and I don’t need confidential records.

### Questions
1. Tell me about the last time you completed this workflow.
2. How many times per week/month does it happen?
3. Which roles and tools are involved?
4. How much elapsed time and hands-on time does it require?
5. What errors, delays, or missed opportunities happen?
6. What happens if it is late or wrong?
7. Have you tried templates, outsourcing, scripts, existing software, or AI tools?
8. What did those alternatives cost and why do they fall short?
9. Who would approve and pay for a change?
10. Could a small test be run on non-sensitive or explicitly approved data?
11. What outcome would justify payment?
12. What would make you refuse to adopt a new solution?

**Potential offer only after discovery:** a tightly scoped, human-reviewed pilot with a written scope, agreed metric, approved data, and no guarantee of results before measurement. Do not provide an unbounded free consulting project.

## Test C — creator-/community-led consumer brand

This track must not assume celebrity access. First test whether a genuine channel or distinctive product insight exists.

### Questions for target consumers
1. What did you last buy in this category, and when?
2. What prompted that purchase?
3. Which alternatives did you consider?
4. What disappointed you about the product or buying experience?
5. How often do you buy it, and what did you last pay?
6. Have you switched brands? Why?
7. What do you currently do to solve the unmet need?
8. Who or what influences your decision, and where do you actually buy?
9. What would make you switch from your current choice?
10. What evidence would you need to trust a new brand?

### Questions for a potential creator/community/retail partner
1. Does the audience match the specific target buyer?
2. What evidence exists of audience trust and purchase conversion in this category?
3. What are the actual terms, fees, rights, and responsibilities?
4. Can the channel support repeated purchases, not only launch attention?
5. What would a small, transparent demand test look like before inventory is bought?
6. What proof would justify scaling?

### Consumer-brand gate
No inventory purchase based only on compliments, polls, followers, or waitlist counts. Before a launch, obtain real supplier quotes, understand category-specific product-safety and labelling obligations, model fulfilment and return/RTO costs, and run a small compliant test with transparent claims.

## Seven-day operating schedule

### Day 1 — access map
List 10 industrial contacts, 10 B2B workflow contacts, and (only if there is a genuine channel path) 5 consumer/creator/retail contacts. Each entry must be a real person or organization the founder can legitimately approach. Do not fabricate names or imply access.

### Days 2–4 — conversations
Target at least 5 completed conversations across the tracks, prioritizing quality over volume. A no-response is not a negative demand signal; it is a distribution/access signal. Record every completed conversation.

### Day 5 — compare repeated patterns
Look for at least three independent people describing a similar problem or desire without being led. Distinguish repeated language from founder interpretation.

### Day 6 — economic sketch
For the two strongest hypotheses, estimate:
- current cost or consequence;
- cost and time to deliver a test;
- likely buyer/customer payment;
- support/operations burden;
- repeatability;
- largest unknown.

Use ranges and label assumptions. Do not fabricate prices or ROI.

### Day 7 — decision review
Advance a track only if it has:
- a specific recurring problem/desire;
- access to the person who experiences it and/or owns the budget;
- a plausible route to test;
- an observable outcome or real purchase behaviour;
- a reason current alternatives are insufficient.

A test with insufficient access is **inconclusive**, not failed. If no track meets the gate, extend discovery or change the target segment; do not build in order to avoid uncertainty.

## Scoring rubric after conversations

Rate each criterion 0–5, but leave it **unknown** where evidence is absent:
1. Problem/desire urgency and frequency
2. Evidence of current cost or actual purchase behaviour
3. Buyer/customer access
4. Willingness to pay / existing spend
5. Data or distribution access
6. Measurability of outcome
7. Advantage over current alternatives
8. Repeatability
9. Plausible contribution economics
10. Founder-specific advantage
11. Capital and downside fit

Do not create an aggregate score if more than one-third of the criteria are unknown. A high total cannot compensate for no buyer access or no evidence of payment.
