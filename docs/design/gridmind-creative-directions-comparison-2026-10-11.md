# GridMind Creative Directions — Comparison Before Prototype

**Date:** 2026-10-11  
**Status:** Concept exploration only. None of these directions is approved for implementation.  
**Precondition:** The buyer/problem brief is a hypothesis until relevant discovery supports it. Any demo must label synthetic data and unverified capabilities.

## Creative objective

Make an industrial buyer understand, within seconds, that GridMind helps investigate a concrete operational question using traceable evidence and evaluate possible value. The experience should feel original and purposeful—not like a generic AI dashboard, energy-monitoring brand, decorative data network, or animated agency site.

The experience should explain a decision, not merely illustrate “intelligence.”

## Direction A — The Case File

**Core metaphor:** An operational mystery investigated through evidence.

**Opening frame:** A specific, plain-language question appears beside a compact set of conflicting operational clues. Example scenario is clearly labelled illustrative: “Why did output fall during this shift?” The interface makes visible which facts are known and which are missing.

**Interaction:** The visitor opens evidence, compares time windows, sees competing explanations, and learns what additional observation would distinguish them. The experience ends with an evidence-backed next step, not a magical AI answer.

**Visual language:** Editorial typography, restrained industrial photography or diagrams, document-like evidence cards, annotated relationships, precise transitions. Avoid detective clichés, red-string boards, and fake terminal text.

**What it communicates:** GridMind helps teams reason through operational ambiguity and keeps evidence separate from assumptions.

**Risks:** Could feel like a consulting report or manual workflow. It needs a compelling, recognizable visual signature and should not imply the real product already has every illustrated feature.

## Direction B — The Decision Instrument

**Core metaphor:** A precision instrument that helps a team move from uncertain signals to a better-supported decision.

**Opening frame:** One operational question with a few visible measurements and a clear uncertainty boundary. The visual hierarchy shows “observed,” “inferred,” and “not yet known” without requiring a long explanation.

**Interaction:** A visitor changes one assumption or selects a comparison window; the estimated impact and confidence change visibly. The demo explains why the result changes and which assumptions drive it. The data and scenario are explicitly synthetic.

**Visual language:** Strong typographic hierarchy, deliberate geometry, calibrated scales, sparse data visualization, tactile but restrained transitions. No generic glowing orb, particle cloud, or fake dashboard wall.

**What it communicates:** GridMind is disciplined, evidence-led, and economically oriented; it exposes uncertainty rather than hiding it.

**Risks:** May feel abstract or overly technical. It depends on accurate explanatory copy and a scenario whose assumptions can be understood quickly.

## Direction C — The System Reveal

**Core metaphor:** A complex industrial process becomes more understandable as relevant relationships are revealed.

**Opening frame:** A single process or value-flow schematic, not a full factory map. Several disconnected signals become linked only when the visitor explores a specific question.

**Interaction:** Selecting a problem highlights the few relevant dependencies, identifies missing evidence, and shows how a hypothesis could be tested. The visual must not imply causality solely because two signals are connected.

**Visual language:** Custom process diagrams, spatial composition, carefully staged reveals, a strong static silhouette, and a restrained palette. Motion reveals relationships and evidence status, not just movement.

**What it communicates:** GridMind helps make complex operations legible across disconnected information.

**Risks:** High risk of looking like a generic network visualization or industrial digital twin. More expensive to make distinctive and accessible. Requires strong discipline to avoid implying that every data source is already integrated.

### Interpretation
**The Case File is the provisional leader for a low-cost storyboard test, not an approved design.** It explains the work GridMind proposes to do without relying on an unproven product capability or decorative motion. The Decision Instrument is a close alternative and may win if buyers respond more strongly to transparent assumptions and economic reasoning. The System Reveal has higher visual distinctiveness potential but a greater risk of generic network imagery and unsupported integration implications.

The scores are subjective priors. They must not be described as validated by customers.

## Test plan before choosing

Show the three static storyboards in randomized order to relevant industrial professionals where possible. Do not explain the intended interpretation first. Ask each person:
1. What do you think this company does?
2. Who would use or pay for it?
3. What business problem does it appear to address?
4. Which part feels credible or not credible, and why?
5. What would you need to see before trusting it?
6. What next step would you actually take?

Capture confusion, incorrect assumptions, and reasons for disinterest—not just preference. If no target buyers are available, use internal review only and label it accordingly.

## Prototype gate

Do not implement a full animated homepage yet. First produce:
- one static storyboard per direction;
- a short, clearly labelled illustrative scenario;
- a plain-language explanation of the evidence and uncertainty;
- a mobile/keyboard/reduced-motion sketch;
- a claim-to-capability checklist.

Advance a direction only if it improves comprehension and commercial credibility without misrepresenting current product capability. If none passes, reject all three and revisit the problem statement rather than forcing a design choice.


## 2026-10-11 — Creative reset after review

**Status:** The previous directions were too abstract to judge as actual website experiences. This section replaces the next-step brief with three more concrete art-direction prototypes. None is approved. The goal is to compare a visual *idea*, a visitor journey, and a memorable signature—not three palettes applied to the same diagram.

### Why the previous pass was insufficient

- It named metaphors but did not define a frame strong enough to recognize from a screenshot.
- Motion timing was specified before the underlying visual composition and story were convincing.
- The concepts converged on the same evidence-card / diagram language.
- The proposed opening line and industrial example were not proven by buyer interviews.
- “Premium,” “cinematic,” and “distinctive” were treated as outcomes instead of measurable design decisions.

The next prototypes must each have a different visual grammar, static silhouette, interaction model, and emotional register. No coding until one direction is selected.

# Prototype 1 — The Hidden Shift
**Creative thesis:** The important story is not that a factory has data. It is that the same shift can look different depending on which operational layer you inspect.

### Screenshot test: opening frame

A wide, editorial canvas with a single continuous horizontal time axis spanning the page. Three carefully aligned bands sit across it:
- **Production context** — what the line was expected to do.
- **Energy behaviour** — what the illustrative measurement shows.
- **Operating events** — what else was happening during that period.

Most of the canvas is quiet. One time window is visually isolated by a narrow vertical bracket. The headline sits above the chart, not over it:

**The number changed. What changed around it?**

Supporting copy: “Industrial signals become useful when they are read in operating context.”

A visible label reads **ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO · SYNTHETIC DATA**. No customer name, live-status badge, or savings number.

### Signature transformation

1. Start with one readable measurement trace.
2. Reveal production context on the same time axis; the first trace is not replaced, it becomes interpretable in context.
3. Reveal a clearly labelled operating event.
4. The selected window expands into a focused inspection area.
5. A side-by-side comparison distinguishes **observed** from **possible explanation**.
6. The story ends with a precise unanswered question: what additional evidence would discriminate between the plausible explanations?

The signature is **context arriving around a stable measurement**. It should be recognizable even in a static sequence of three screenshots. No flying particles, glowing connections, or decorative grid.

### Interaction

The visitor selects one of two time windows. The evidence bands align to the chosen period; an explanation panel states what is directly visible and what remains unknown. The interaction must be deterministic and the illustrative data internally consistent.

### Art direction

- Base: warm white / mineral grey.
- Text: near-black.
- One accent: deep vermilion or safety-orange used only for the selected interval and active evidence.
- Typography: assertive editorial headline; compact, highly legible technical labels.
- Shapes: precise bands, brackets, tick marks, and annotations; no floating card wall.
- Photography: optional and secondary; the time-aligned evidence is the hero.

### Mobile / accessibility

On mobile, the bands become vertically stacked but share one labelled time range; allow horizontal panning only for the data region, never the whole page. Provide a table/text equivalent. Reduced motion displays the complete aligned state immediately. Keyboard users can change the selected window and inspect each band.

### What it proves—and does not prove

It demonstrates a *method of contextual investigation*. It does not prove that GridMind has live integrations, can identify root causes autonomously, or has quantified a real factory loss.

### Buyer question

“Can this help my team stop interpreting an operational metric without its context?”

### Main risk

Could feel like a sophisticated data-visualization consultancy. The copy and transition into a concrete decision must show why context changes the investigation.

# Prototype 2 — The Decision Under Glass
**Creative thesis:** Put the decision on the table first. Let the visitor inspect the assumptions that support—or weaken—it.

### Screenshot test: opening frame

A large, gallery-like composition shows one industrial decision as a physical-feeling, typographic object: **Investigate / Adjust / Leave unchanged**. Beneath it is a thin horizontal evidence ledger, not a dashboard. Three columns read **Known**, **Assumed**, and **Missing**.

The hero headline:

**Before you act, know what the evidence can support.**

The visual signature is a transparent, layered decision sheet: the visitor can pull apart the basis of a proposed decision rather than being shown an opaque AI answer.

### Signature transformation

1. A provisional decision appears in a quiet central field.
2. Three evidence layers align underneath it: observations, assumptions, missing information.
3. The visitor opens one assumption; its source and consequence are shown.
4. The decision status changes from “ready” to “needs evidence” (or vice versa only when the documented scenario justifies it).
5. The final frame is not a score or magic recommendation. It is a clear next step and a visible rationale.

### Interaction

Choose one of two hypothetical operating responses. The comparison reveals which evidence supports each option, which trade-offs are unknown, and what should be checked before deciding. Do not calculate fake ROI or manufacture confidence percentages. If an actual scenario model is not validated, the interaction remains qualitative.

### Art direction

- Base: pale limestone or warm grey.
- Text: dark graphite.
- Accent: cobalt used for the selected evidence layer; amber for unresolved questions.
- Typography: editorial headline with a rigorous tabular/technical secondary system.
- Composition: central decision sheet and horizontal evidence ledger; no generic metric cards.
- Motion: layers align, separate, and settle. No dramatic numerical counters.

### Mobile / accessibility

Convert layers to an ordered disclosure: decision, supporting evidence, assumptions, missing information. All states are readable without animation. Keyboard and touch use explicit “Inspect” controls.

### What it proves—and does not prove

It communicates accountable decision support and transparent reasoning. It must not imply automated approval, a validated recommendation engine, or quantified business impact unless those are real capabilities.

### Buyer question

“Will GridMind help us understand why a decision is justified—and what still needs checking?”

### Main risk

It may be too abstract for a first-time visitor unless the industrial decision is immediately concrete and recognizable.

# Prototype 3 — The Operating Portrait
**Creative thesis:** Let the visitor see an industrial process as a living operational composition—not as a web of nodes or a stock-photo backdrop.

### Screenshot test: opening frame

A custom, orthographic illustration of one hypothetical production cell occupies most of the viewport. It is not a 3D factory fly-through. It is a clean, cutaway-style portrait: a few machines, a utility path, material flow, and a small control/measurement layer.

The headline:

**Understand the operation behind the number.**

At first, only the process itself is visible. Small labels establish the major stages. When the visitor chooses “Explore a change,” the view does not zoom theatrically; the composition changes focus, showing only the relevant part of the process and the observations that would help investigate it.

The static silhouette should be ownable and recognizable in a screenshot.

### Signature transformation

1. Show the whole simplified process, with no animated data noise.
2. Highlight one operational stage and the flow entering/leaving it.
3. Reveal two relevant observations in context.
4. Focus the composition on the selected stage while retaining a miniature overview.
5. Present a question, evidence boundary, and next investigation—not a definitive root-cause claim.

### Interaction

Select a process stage. The illustration highlights the stage, shows relevant illustrative observations, and explains what context or additional evidence is needed. Selection must never imply that all depicted equipment is connected to a real GridMind installation.

### Art direction

- Base: deep ink-blue or charcoal, with a light neutral illustration.
- Accent: one warm industrial amber; a second status colour only if meaningfully necessary.
- Typography: clear sans-serif, large plain-language headline, small but accessible technical annotations.
- Geometry: solid planes, restrained outlines, purposeful depth; no glowing node network.
- Camera: fixed orthographic composition; no spinning or cinematic fly-through.

### Mobile / accessibility

On mobile, the portrait becomes a labelled vertical process sequence with expandable stages. Offer a text list and clear selection state. Reduced motion shows the completed diagram and updates selection immediately.

### What it proves—and does not prove

It makes the wider operational context tangible. It does not prove that GridMind has a digital twin, live equipment map, or integrations with the depicted machinery.

### Buyer question

“Can GridMind help us investigate one issue without losing sight of the wider operation?”

### Main risk

Most visually ambitious and most expensive to execute well. If the illustration lacks industrial specificity, it will look like an attractive but generic process graphic.

### Provisional recommendation: prototype Hidden Shift first

This is a narrow recommendation, not approval. It has the clearest explanation of why industrial context matters, a strong static composition, and a feasible first interaction without inventing a product capability. Decision Under Glass is the best challenger if GridMind’s positioning is primarily about accountable decisions. Operating Portrait is the best challenger if the brand needs a more iconic visual asset and the team can afford custom illustration.

### Required test before committing

Create one static opening frame and a three-frame sequence for each candidate. Show them in randomized order to at least a few relevant industrial buyers if possible, without explaining the intended meaning. Record:
1. What they think GridMind does.
2. What they believe the visual claims.
3. What they find credible or suspicious.
4. Whether they understand the next step.
5. Which concept would make them continue—and why.

Do not use likes or “looks cool” as the decision metric. If no buyers are available, label the review internal and keep the decision provisional.

### Implementation gate

No homepage-wide animation and no replacement of the existing product route. After a direction is selected, prototype only the hero-to-investigation transition, test desktop/mobile/reduced-motion/keyboard behavior, and compare comprehension before extending the visual language across the site.
